# 5 Sharia Screening Rules to Spot Risky, Over-Leveraged Stocks

A company with a perfectly permissible business — manufacturing, technology, healthcare — can still fail Sharia screening.

The reason isn't its product. It's its balance sheet. And that's the irony most people miss: these five rules, though entirely rooted in Islamic jurisprudence, function in practice as a strict balance-sheet quality filter — the kind financial analysts with no connection to jurisprudence use routinely.

## Why These Rules Matter Even If Compliance Isn't Your First Criterion

A company carrying heavy interest-bearing debt pays that interest regardless of how it performs. One slowdown, or one rate hike, and what looked like strong growth becomes a liquidity crisis.

AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 21 caps that exposure. The practical result: a portfolio less fragile under shocks — a benefit that requires no religious motivation to appreciate.

## Rule 1: Interest-Bearing Debt vs. Market Cap

The ceiling under AAOIFI Standard No. 21: **total interest-bearing debt must stay under 30%** of average market capitalization.

Other methodologies (FTSE, DJIM) use roughly a third (33.3%). The gap is minor in practice; the principle is identical.

For most investors, the practical route is to check a ready-made compliance tag from an established screening tool (Zoya, Musaffa) rather than calculating manually. For anyone wanting to verify independently, the formula is direct: total interest-bearing debt ÷ average market cap over 36 months — recalculated quarterly, since the number moves with every new filing.

## Rule 2: Cash and Interest-Bearing Assets

Same ceiling (30% of market cap), but on the opposite side of the balance sheet: cash and interest-generating securities.

This rule is less well-known than the first, and smarter than it looks. It catches companies that appear "safe" on the surface — sitting on large cash reserves — while that same cash is parked in interest-bearing deposits. Conservative in appearance, not in substance.

## Rule 3: Income From Non-Permissible Sources

The ceiling here is far tighter: **5% of total revenue** maximum from prohibited activities (interest, gambling, alcohol, tobacco, conventional insurance).

The narrow margin is deliberate. It permits incidental, unintentional exposure — modest interest on an operating bank account that any company needs — while firmly rejecting any company where prohibited activity forms a real part of its business model.

## Rule 4: Business Activity Is Decided Before Any Number

Before any ratio is calculated, a company is excluded outright if its core business is prohibited: conventional banking, alcohol, gambling, weapons, adult content.

A company with pristine financial ratios whose business is selling alcohol is out before the math begins. The qualitative screen always precedes the quantitative one, and no number is good enough to override it.

## Rule 5: Screening Has an Expiry Date

This is the rule most beginners skip, and practically the most dangerous.

The ratios above are calculated from the latest audited financials and re-checked on a regular cycle — usually quarterly. A company that passed today may fail after its next earnings report if debt rises or its revenue mix shifts. Some methodologies (FTSE's, for instance) apply a "buffer zone" around the threshold to avoid a stock flipping status constantly while hovering near the line.

**Do this now:** set a recurring quarterly reminder to re-check every holding's status. Two minutes every three months prevents a painful discovery a year later.

## Mistakes That Repeat When Applying These Rules

**Stopping at the business screen.** Many stop at "the business is permissible, so it's fine" — missing that a fully permissible business can still fail on excessive leverage.

**Mixing methodologies.** AAOIFI, FTSE, and S&P use slightly different thresholds (30% vs. 33.3%). Know which one your tool applies before building a decision on its number.

**Treating a screening result as permanent.** A result that's a year old may be flatly wrong today.

## FAQ

**Are these rules only for Muslim investors?**
Their origin is jurisprudential, but their practical effect — avoiding over-leveraged companies — benefits any investor trying to reduce structural risk in a portfolio.

**Why does the threshold differ between 30% and 33.3%?**
Each standard-setting body (AAOIFI, FTSE, DJIM, S&P) arrived at its threshold through accepted jurisprudential and financial reasoning. The differences are minor and don't change the underlying principle.

**Is one screening check at purchase enough?**
No. Re-screen at least quarterly, since financial ratios move with every report.

**What if a company hovers right at the threshold?**
Some methodologies apply a buffer zone to avoid constant flipping. But as a practical rule, consistently sitting near the ceiling warrants extra caution regardless of the official classification.
